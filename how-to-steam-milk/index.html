How to Steam Milk

Good steamed milk adds more than heat. Fine, evenly mixed bubbles give a latte its silky texture and a cappuccino its soft foam. Learn how to steam milk with an espresso machine, build microfoam, and fix common texture problems at home.

Simply heating milk does not create microfoam. You need a little air, introduced early, followed by a rolling flow that blends the bubbles into the milk. Aim for glossy, pourable texture rather than a dry cap of foam.

Quick answer: how to steam milk

  1. Start with cold milk in a clean pitcher, roughly one-third to halfway full, with room to expand.
  2. Bring the machine to steaming readiness and briefly purge the wand into the drip tray.
  3. Place the tip just below the surface, slightly off-centre, then turn on the steam.
  4. Introduce air briefly with a gentle paper-tearing sound.
  5. Submerge the tip a little farther and keep the milk rolling in a vortex.
  6. Stop around 60–65 °C (roughly 140–150 °F). Turn the steam off before removing the pitcher.
  7. Wipe the wand immediately with a damp cloth and purge again.
  8. Tap lightly if needed, swirl until glossy, and pour immediately.
Two phases: add air early, then blend it in. Adding air until the milk is hot usually creates coarse or dry foam.

What good steamed milk should look like

Microfoam has very fine bubbles, a glossy surface, and a flowing consistency similar to liquid paint. Swirl the pitcher: the milk and foam should move together rather than leave a stiff raft on top of thin liquid.

A latte needs a thinner, integrated texture. Cappuccino milk can have more volume while still being smooth and pourable. Visible large bubbles and spoon-stiff foam are signs to adjust your technique.

Equipment and setup

  • Espresso machine with a steam wand: follow its instructions for steam mode and readiness.
  • Stainless steel milk pitcher: leave room for circulation and expansion.
  • Cold milk: measure only what you need for the drink.
  • Thermometer: optional, but useful while learning when to stop.
  • Clean damp cloth: keep it ready for wiping the wand immediately.

This technique is for a conventional steam tip. An air-assisted frothing sleeve or automatic steam system may require different positioning; use the machine’s instructions. Choosing your first setup? Step by step: make microfoam with a steam wand

1. Measure cold milk and leave headroom

Start roughly one-third to halfway up the pitcher, often near the bottom of the spout. For example, 150–200 mL can be a useful starting amount in a 400 mL pitcher, depending on its shape and your drink. Stay within marked fill limits. Too little milk makes it difficult to cover the tip; too much leaves no room for foam or movement.

2. Ready the machine and purge

Wait for steam readiness. Point the wand into the drip tray and briefly release steam to clear condensed water, keeping hands away from the outlet. Close the valve before positioning the pitcher. Weak or watery steam makes the next steps harder; do not compensate by adding air throughout the heating cycle.

3. Set the tip just below the surface

Tilt the pitcher slightly and position the wand off-centre, between the centre and the wall. Keep the tip just submerged, rather than against the bottom. Turn on steam as directed by the manufacturer, using a steady output. Adjust the angle until the milk begins to roll around the pitcher.

4. Add a little air while the milk is still cold

Lower the pitcher a few millimetres so the tip sits at the surface and gently draws in air. Listen for a soft, intermittent paper-tearing sound. Loud spluttering or spraying means the tip is too exposed: raise the pitcher slightly. For a latte, stop adding air after a modest increase in volume; for a cappuccino, allow a little more expansion.

Use the volume change as feedback, not a fixed number of seconds. Steam power, milk quantity, tip design, and milk formulation all change how quickly this phase happens.

5. Blend with a rolling vortex

Raise the pitcher slightly to put the tip farther below the surface. Repeated air intake should stop while the milk keeps rolling. Make small angle or sideways adjustments until the surface circulates smoothly. This motion distributes bubbles through the liquid; adding more air is not a substitute for it.

6. Watch temperature and shut off first

Aim to finish around 60–65 °C (roughly 140–150 °F). A thermometer gives more useful feedback than guessing from touch. Allow for a slow-reading thermometer and residual heating: you may need to close the valve slightly before the target. Keep the tip submerged until the steam has stopped, then lower and remove the pitcher.

7. Clean, swirl, and pour

Immediately wipe the wand with your clean damp cloth, then briefly purge into the drip tray. If a few large bubbles remain, tap the pitcher lightly on a stable surface. Swirl until the texture looks glossy and uniform. Tapping cannot rescue an entire pitcher of coarse foam.

Pour into prepared espresso promptly, gently swirling if you need a short pause. Long waits encourage separation. For a better espresso base, follow how to dial in espresso; our espresso grinder guide explains the equipment side of repeatable shots.

Milk steaming temperature

A practical finishing range is 60–65 °C, or approximately 140–150 °F. More precisely, 65 °C is 149 °F; the rounded range is easier to use at the machine. This is a texture and drinking-temperature guideline. Follow any specific directions for your milk or machine.

Overheating can bring a cooked flavour and less pleasant texture. Extra swirling will not undo it. Start again with fresh cold milk and stop earlier. For plant-based drinks, check the carton for a suggested steaming range.

Latte vs cappuccino: change the amount of air

How to steam milk for a latte

Add a small amount of air early, then spend the rest of the heating phase integrating it. Aim for thin, silky microfoam that pours as one liquid.

How to steam milk for cappuccino

Introduce a little more air early for greater foam volume, then keep the vortex going. More foam should still mean smooth texture, not a dry mound separate from the milk.

Which milk works for steaming?

Results depend on protein, fat, formulation, and technique. Treat these as starting expectations, not guarantees across every brand.

  • Whole milk: a useful starting point for a rich, creamy texture.
  • 2% milk: can produce fine foam with a lighter mouthfeel than whole milk.
  • Skim milk: can build plenty of foam, but may feel less rich. Avoid excessive aeration.
  • Oat: many barista versions are formulated for steaming; performance varies by brand.
  • Almond: some formulations make less stable foam. Start with gentle aeration and check carton guidance.
  • Soy: can foam well, but formulation and interaction with coffee affect the result.

Some “barista” versions are designed to foam more easily. If one carton separates despite a good vortex and sensible temperature, try another formulation before making large technique changes.

Clean the steam wand after every pitcher

Wipe immediately with a clean damp cloth and briefly purge into the drip tray. Do not let milk dry on the tip. The wand and discharged steam are hot; keep fingers away from the outlet and use the insulated grip if fitted.

Routine wiping does not replace deeper cleaning. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for the tip, cleaning products, and removable frothing sleeve. If steam flow remains weak or uneven, check for blocked holes using the approved cleaning method.

FAQ

What temperature should steamed milk be?
Aim to finish around 60–65 °C (roughly 140–150 °F). Use a thermometer while learning and account for reading lag. Follow specific guidance for your milk or machine.
How long should you steam milk?
There is no universal time. Steam power, milk amount, and starting temperature change the timing. Add air early, then texture until you reach the target temperature.
Why does my milk have large bubbles?
The tip may be too exposed, or you may be adding air too late. Introduce air gently while the milk is cold, then submerge the tip slightly and maintain a rolling vortex.
Can you steam oat milk?
Yes, but results vary by formulation. Some barista oat drinks are designed for steaming. Check the carton’s temperature guidance and adjust aeration to the texture you see.
What is the difference between steamed milk and frothed milk?
Steaming uses steam to heat milk and can create integrated microfoam. Frothing describes adding air; a frother may produce a thicker foam layer, and some frothers also heat milk. Texture depends on the tool and technique.
How much milk should I put in the pitcher?
Start roughly one-third to halfway full, leaving room for expansion. Around 150–200 mL in a 400 mL pitcher is an example, not a fixed rule. Follow marked fill limits and adjust for pitcher shape and drink size.
Why does milk scream when steaming?
A tip held too deep with little air introduced is a common cause. Poor circulation or steam readiness may contribute. While the milk is cold, bring the tip nearer the surface, add a little air, and establish a vortex.
